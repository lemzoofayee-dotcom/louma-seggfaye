Cymbium: the sea snail Senegal calls yeet

By Lamine Faye, the Guedjologue Published September 28, 2026 Reading time: 4 min

In short: cymbium is a large sea snail from the coasts of West Africa. In Senegal we call it yeet (also written yett, yette or yet). Dried and fermented, it becomes the seasoning that gives thieboudienne its taste. And its fishing is regulated by law: nothing under 17.5 cm.

Search for “cymbium” and you land on scientific pages: Cymbium pepo, Cymbium glans, the volute family. All correct, but they miss the point: in Senegal this shell is not a museum curiosity. It is everyday food, and I sell it.

What exactly is cymbium?

Cymbium is a gastropod mollusc: a big sea snail, if you like. It lives buried in the sand, in shallow water along the coast, from Senegal down to Angola.

There are several species. The largest, Cymbium glans, grows beyond 30 cm and can weigh several kilos. Cymbium pepo lives further north. Fishermen don't bother with Latin: for everyone, it is yeet.

Cymbium or yeet: one animal, two words

Cymbium is the genus name used by scientists. Yeet is its name in Wolof, the main language of Senegal. Wolof is spoken more than it is written, so everyone spells it by ear: yett, yette or yet. It is the same shellfish.

Why we don't take the small ones: the 17.5 cm rule

In Senegal, the law forbids catching cymbium under 17.5 cm, measured from the tip of the shell to its lowest point. Below that size the animal has not had time to reproduce. Catching it means eating tomorrow's harvest.

In the Îles du Saloum (the Saloum Delta), communities go further than the law. From June to August, they close fishing areas themselves, led above all by the women. Nobody forces them: it is a biological rest. The sea rests, the yeet grows.

Science is looking at it too. In 2023 and 2024, Senegalese and French researchers (ISRA-CRODT and the LEMAR laboratory in Brest, with JICA) studied cymbium growth in tanks at Ouakam and Pointe Sarène, to prepare a management plan and test farming.

The taste: why it is called the African Maggi

Fresh, cymbium flesh is as tender as butter. Dried and fermented, it develops a very powerful umami taste, deep and briny: the same family of flavours as fermented anchovies or Japanese dashi.

One small piece flavours a whole pot. That is why we call it the African Maggi: it does the job of a stock cube, naturally, with no additives. Our grandmothers used it long before stock cubes reached Africa.

Which dishes it goes into

Dried cymbium goes first into thieboudienne, Senegal's national dish, listed by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage in 2021. You also find it in caldou (fish broth), soupou kandja (okra sauce) and mbakhalou Saloum.

The rule never changes: crumble it into the sauce at the start of cooking, it melts and flavours the whole dish. And salt afterwards, never before: it already brings salt.
